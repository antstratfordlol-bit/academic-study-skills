# Questions, by stage and discipline

For the `socratic-mentor` skill. **Choose; do not recite.** Ask the one that fits what the student has just said.
Each stage mixes four kinds: *clarifying* (what exactly do you mean), *probing* (how do you know), *structuring*
(what must come first), and *challenging* (what would someone who disagrees say).

## 0 — Where are you

- What have you read so far, and what have you only heard about?
- Can you say your question in one sentence yet? If not, what is the nearest you can get?
- What do your faculty's norms require: length, language, citation style, the file you submit?
- When do you next see your supervisor, and what do they expect you to bring?

## 1 — The question

- *Prediction:* what do you think will be hardest about narrowing this down?
- If you swapped the nouns in your question for any other subject's, would it still read? Then it is a topic. What
  about *this* subject makes it a question?
- What would count as an answer? What would the last paragraph of the thesis say, in outline?
- Could it be answered in the length you have? What would you cut first?
- Why now? What makes this question open, or newly answerable?

## 2 — The thesis

- State what you will show, in one sentence, with no "explore" or "examine".
- Is it a claim about what a text says, about what happened, or about what is true? Each needs different evidence.
- Are you saying A causes B, A explains B, A is compatible with B, or A develops into B?
- Where does your claim stop? Name one case it does not cover.

## 3 — Status quaestionis

- *Prediction:* on a scale of one to ten, how complete is your reading of the literature? Where is it thin?
- Name the two or three works closest to yours. What does each leave unanswered, specifically?
- Where did you search: which databases, which catalogues, up to when? What would you type to find a study that
  answers your question? If the search returns nothing, that is your gap, bounded by that search.
- Is there a reading you disagree with? Can you state it so its author would accept the summary?

## 4 — Sources and method

- Which texts carry the argument? Which edition and which language of each, and why that one?
- Are you reading a translation where the argument turns on a word? What does the original say?
- Which method fits your kind of claim, and why that one rather than the obvious alternative?
- What would make you wrong? What evidence would change your mind?

## 5 — The chapters

For each chapter:

- What must the reader understand by the end of it?
- What is its one claim, and which passages carry it?
- *Prediction:* what will your supervisor question first here?
- Why does it come before the next one? What would break if you swapped them?

## 6 — The strongest objection

- Who holds the opposite reading, and on what texts?
- State their objection better than they did. Would they sign it?
- What is your answer, and does it concede anything? What exactly?
- Is there a simpler explanation of your evidence than yours?

## 7 — The contribution

- After reading your thesis, what will a specialist know that they did not before?
- Is that a new text, a new reading, a new connection, or a new synthesis?
- Is "the first to" true within the searches you ran, and have you said which searches?

## 8 — The defence

- What is the question you most hope the examiners will not ask? Answer it now.
- Explain your thesis in two minutes to a specialist, then to an intelligent friend.
- Which of your own claims is weakest, and how would you defend it if pressed?

## By discipline

**Theology (systematic, moral, spiritual).** What is the authority of each text you cite, and does your argument
depend on reading more authority into it than it carries? How does the question stand in Scripture, the Fathers,
the Magisterium and the theologians, and which of these carries your argument? Is your claim a matter of faith, of
common teaching, or of theological opinion, and does your wording match? For moral theology: what are the object,
end and circumstances in your case, and on what text does each rest?

**Philosophy.** Define your key term as your author uses it, then as you use it. Are they the same? Reconstruct the
argument as premises and a conclusion; which premise does the work, and is it true? Is your reading of the author
historical (what they meant) or systematic (whether it holds)? Keep the two apart.

**Biblical studies.** Which text-critical questions affect your passage? Which version are you reading, and does its
numbering differ? What does the passage mean in its own context, before any later reading? Which method
(historical-critical, narrative, canonical, patristic reception) answers your question, and what can it not see?

**History, including the history of Opus Dei and of spirituality.** Which of your sources are contemporary with the
events, and which are recollections? Who wrote each, when, and for whom? Where a published history carries your
point, what archive does its author cite, and can you reach it? Do you attribute each finding to the historian who
made it, not to an institution?

**Canon law.** Which canon, in which version of the Code, and has it been amended? What do the sources of the canon
and the authentic interpretations say? Is your case covered by the letter, by analogy, or not at all?

**Other disciplines.** Ask what counts as evidence in that field, and hold the student to it.
